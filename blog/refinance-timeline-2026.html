Refinance Timeline: How Long It Really Takes in 2026

Michael Harrington · June 13, 2026

Day 1: You apply for a refinance. Day 45: You close. Or maybe day 60. Or maybe day 90.

The timeline is one of the biggest sources of frustration for homeowners. You want the lower payment now. You want to stop worrying about rates. You want it done.

I get it. But refinancing takes time. Let me walk you through the real timeline in 2026—what to expect, where delays happen, and how to speed things up.

I’ll use a real client, Stephanie, whose refinance took 52 days from application to funding. She was annoyed, but she closed. Here’s what happened each week.

Week 1: Application and Documentation (Days 1-7)

What happens: You complete a loan application (online or with a loan officer). You submit documents: pay stubs, W-2s or tax returns, bank statements, and a copy of your most recent mortgage statement.

Stephanie’s experience: She submitted everything on Day 1. But her bank statements had a $1,000 deposit from her mom that the underwriter questioned. She had to write a letter explaining it. That took an extra 3 days.

Pro tip: Gather everything before you apply. Most lenders have a checklist. Get it all ready. Large deposits ($1,000+) need explanations. Be ready to write a letter.

Week 2: Rate Lock and Initial Disclosure (Days 8-14)

What happens: You lock your rate (if you haven’t already). The lender sends a Loan Estimate (LE) and initial disclosures. You sign them electronically.

Stephanie’s experience: She locked on Day 8 at 6.4%. On Day 11, rates dropped to 6.3%. She had a float‑down option and asked for it. The lender agreed. New rate: 6.3%. That took 2 days to process.

Pro tip: Lock when you see a rate that works for your budget. Ask about float‑down options before locking.

Week 3: Underwriting Begins (Days 15-21)

What happens: The loan goes to underwriting. The underwriter reviews your file, checks your credit, verifies your income, and orders an appraisal (if required).

Stephanie’s experience: The underwriter came back on Day 18 with conditions: a letter explaining a gap in employment from 2023 (she was in school), a copy of her divorce decree (to prove no alimony obligations), and a clearer copy of a bank statement page that was blurry.

She was annoyed. “Why didn’t they ask for all this at once?” she asked.

Because underwriters are human. They review documents sequentially. Sometimes they miss things.

Pro tip: Respond to conditions within 24 hours. Delays compound.

Week 4: Appraisal and Additional Conditions (Days 22-28)

What happens: The appraisal is ordered. An appraiser visits your home (or does a desktop appraisal). The report takes 5-7 days to come back.

Stephanie’s experience: Her appraisal was ordered on Day 15. The appraiser visited on Day 20. The report came back on Day 26. Value: $425,000, which was $5,000 less than she expected. LTV went from 68% to 71%. Still fine, but close to the 70% threshold.

She considered challenging the appraisal but decided it wasn’t worth the delay. Good call.

Pro tip: Be present during the appraisal. Point out improvements. Make sure the appraiser sees everything.

Week 5: Conditional Approval and Clearing Conditions (Days 29-35)

What happens: The underwriter issues a conditional approval. There are still conditions to clear—usually final documents like the signed appraisal, updated bank statements, or a verification of employment.

Stephanie’s experience: She got conditional approval on Day 30. The remaining conditions were simple: a verbal verification of employment from her employer (they did it the same day) and an updated bank statement showing she hadn’t opened new credit (she sent it on Day 32).

By Day 35, all conditions were cleared. She was ready to close.

Week 6: Closing and Funding (Days 36-45)

What happens: You receive the Closing Disclosure (CD), which shows final numbers. You sign closing documents (usually at a title company or with a mobile notary). Then there’s a 3-day mandatory rescission period for refinances (not for purchases). After that, the loan funds.

Stephanie’s experience: She got her CD on Day 38. She reviewed it, noticed a $200 fee that wasn’t on the Loan Estimate, and asked about it. The lender corrected it. She signed on Day 42. The rescission period ended on Day 45. Funds were disbursed on Day 46. Her old loan was paid off on Day 52 (because of a weekend delay).

She closed, but it took 52 days, not 45. She was a little frustrated, but she got her 6.3% rate and her payment dropped by $180 a month.

The Most Common Delays (And How to Avoid Them)

  • Missing documents: Gather everything before you apply. Use a cloud folder.
  • Employment verification: Tell your HR department a verification call is coming. Give them permission to talk to the lender.
  • Appraisal issues: Make sure the appraiser can access your home. Clean up. Point out upgrades.
  • Title issues: Old liens, judgments, or ownership disputes. Check your title report early.
  • Underwriter questions: Respond immediately. Don’t wait.
  • Rate lock expiration: If your lock expires before closing, you might pay a fee or get a worse rate. Always ask for a lock long enough for your expected timeline plus a buffer.

How to Speed Up Your Refinance

  • Respond to every request within 24 hours.
  • Use a local lender or a digital lender known for speed.
  • Don’t open new credit cards or make large purchases during the process.
  • Check your credit report for errors before you apply.
  • Ask your loan officer for a realistic timeline. Add 10 days to whatever they say.

The Average Timeline in 2026

Based on data from ICE Mortgage Technology, the average refinance in June 2026 takes 45 days from application to funding. 30% close in 30-40 days. 50% close in 40-55 days. 20% take longer than 55 days.

Stephanie’s 52-day timeline was normal. Annoying, but normal.
